A1

Human or Machine?

00:00–08:00

A student wrote something. Then somebody gave feedback. Was it a teacher, or an AI? Vote, then see what the room thinks.

Presenter script

Good morning, everyone. A quick confession first: I'm not an education researcher. I'm a computer scientist. I build and study the kind of systems that are now turning up in your classrooms. So I won't tell you how to teach. I'll show you what these systems are actually good at, where they break, and how they can take work off your desk.

Point to the QR code. Wait about 30 seconds while people join.

First game: Human or Machine. You'll see a student's sentence and some feedback on it. Decide who wrote the feedback: a teacher or an AI. About twenty seconds a round.

Run the 4 rounds. Before each reveal, ask: "Hands up for teacher… hands up for AI." Then press the answer on the projected screen and "Next round".

Look at the pattern. The AI-style feedback is fast, polite, complete and correct. It fixes everything for you. The teacher-style feedback fixes less, but asks the one question that makes the student think. Keep that difference in mind, because it is the whole talk in one slide.

Ask two or three people: "What gave it away? What made you guess AI?" Collect the clues: too polite, fixes everything, could fit any essay.

Note for you, not the audience: the samples were prepared for this workshop to show typical patterns. Round 4 is deliberately ambiguous; use it to say "a well-prompted AI can do this too, but someone still has to check it".

A2

Myth or Fact: the speed quiz

08:00–20:00

Eight questions, 20 seconds each. Faster correct answers score more. We play it live in Kahoot: go to kahoot.it and enter the PIN on the screen. If Kahoot is down, play the version below on your phone.

Presenter script

Launch the Kahoot you imported (see Kahoot setup). Show the PIN.

Time for a quiz. Go to kahoot dot it, type the PIN, pick a nickname, any nickname. Speed counts, and so does being right.

About 7 minutes to play. Spend the last 5 minutes on questions 1, 4 and 7. Backup if Kahoot fails: everyone plays the version on this page and shouts out their score.

Question 1: will AI replace university teachers? Most of you said myth. Good. Here's the engineering reason.

Question 4: hallucination. The model gives you something false, in perfect English, with total confidence. It doesn't know it's wrong, because it doesn't "know" anything in the way we do.

Question 7: how does a language model work? It doesn't look anything up. It predicts the next word, technically the next token, from patterns in a huge amount of text. That one fact explains both sides of the story: why it writes fluent, natural English, and why it can invent a reference with total confidence. It is optimised to sound right, not to be right.

So the useful mental model is this: AI is a very fast, very well-read intern. Tireless, never bored, sometimes confidently wrong. You'd let an intern draft thirty vocabulary questions. You would not let an intern sign off a thesis grade.

TALK

Three jobs for AI in a university

20:00–30:00

The repetitive layer of teaching moves to AI. The judgement layer stays with people, and gets more of their time.

LECTURE DESIGN

Efficient lecture design

First drafts of outlines, worked examples, readings at three levels, exercise banks. AI drafts; the teacher edits and decides.

ASSESSMENT

Standardised assessment

The same rubric applied the same way to essay 1 and essay 120, at 9 a.m. or 2 a.m. First-pass feedback and consistency checks. The final grade stays human.

TUTORING

Personalised academic tutoring

A patient practice partner for every student, any hour, at their level: speaking practice, writing questions, reading support.

Presenter script

Here is the one idea I'd like you to take home. AI does not replace university teachers. It frees them from repetitive, trivial work, so they can focus on what only they can do: academic guidance, critical thinking, and research teaching.

In computer science we've lived through this already. Compilers, then autocomplete, now AI coding assistants. Nobody fired the programmers. The boring part got automated, and the job moved up a level: design, review, judgement. Teaching is going through the same shift right now.

Concretely, AI has three jobs in a university. One: efficient lecture design. It drafts your outline, your examples, a reading at B1, B2 and C1. You edit. Two: standardised assessment. It applies your rubric the same way to every essay and gives first-pass feedback. It never gets tired, and it never gets kinder after lunch. You make the final call. Three: personalised tutoring. Every student gets a patient speaking partner at midnight. That was never possible with one teacher and eighty students.

And notice what's not on that list: deciding whether a research idea is original, mentoring a student who wants to quit, teaching someone to doubt a source. That's the part of the job we're freeing time for.

Pair talk, 3 minutes: "Turn to your neighbour. Which of these three jobs would save you the most time this semester, and on what task?" Then take two or three answers from the room.

A3

Hand it over? The task card sort

30:00–45:00

Twelve real teaching tasks. Sort each one: give it to AI, let AI draft while you decide, or keep it human. Then compare with the person next to you and find one card you disagree on.

AI · hand it to AIAI + me · AI drafts, I decideHuman · keep it human
Presenter script

Twelve task cards. For each one, three choices: hand it to AI, let AI draft while you decide, or keep it human. Four minutes. There's no right answer, and that's the point.

4 min sort · 4 min pairs · 7 min room discussion.

Now show your phone to the person next to you. Find one card where you disagree, and convince each other. Four minutes.

Then read out cards 3, 4, 6 and 8 one at a time. For each: "Hands up AI… AI plus me… human." Stop on the card where the room splits most and ask one person from each side to explain.

The unanimous ones are boring: nobody wants AI mentoring a student who wants to quit. The split ones are interesting. Who put "first-pass feedback on 120 essays" in Human? Why? … And who put it in AI? … This disagreement is exactly the conversation each department needs to have, task by task. The middle column, "AI drafts, I decide", is where most of the real gains are.
